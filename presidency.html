The First President of the United States

George Washington was unanimously elected by the Electoral College into the office of president on April 30th, 1789. Many functions and responsibilities of the office we can attribute to him, like the two term limit. It can also be said that we do not have a monarchy, at least in part, to Washington. Back in 1782, Colonel Lewis Nicola had written a letter to Washington about the failures of Congress and the idea of a monarchy, with Washington filling the role of king. Washington had strong feelings on the matter and sternly declined the offer. Washington helped to organize both the federal government and the nation's finances. While Washington's presidency was a time of building up the nation after a time of war, war would not remain distant for long.

The Whiskey Rebellion

In true American fashion the Whiskey Rebellion from 1791 to 1794, was spurred on by a federal tax. Under the Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton, a tax was passed on whiskey to raise funds and pay off debts. Western farmers had a problem with this new tax as they felt it unfairly targeted them due to whiskey being a large part of their economy. Protests began which quickly became violent and included the tarring and feathering of various government officials like tax collectors. The peak of this rebellion occurred when armed men attacked and burned the home of John Neville who was a tax man. As diplomacy failed, Washington raised and led an army one last time, which happens to be the one and only case of a president of the United States leading troops. Upon arrival of Washington and his army the large group of rebels retreated and disbanded without a battle being fought.

Washington's Farewell

In 1796, George Washington gave his Farewell Address, his leaving after the end of his second term set the precedent for how long a president should serve. During his address he warned against the nation becoming divided by politics or political party and urged for national unity. Washington wanted to end Americans putting regional interests above national ones. Washington also urged against permanent alliances with foreign powers, which could lead to the country being drawn into foreign wars. Most of all, he wanted to maintain American independence and national unity of the people. With his address, Washington stepped away from central power peacefully and retired to Mount Vernon. While he did temporarily rejoin the military when tensions were high with France, he would never return to combat or to command in the field. On December 14th of 1799, Washington passed away at his Mount Vernon estate at the age of sixty-seven.
